How to Find Your Core Values (and Actually Live by Them)

Most people can rattle off a few values (honesty, family, freedom) borrowed from a list and hoped to be true. But when life gets noisy, borrowed words collapse. Your core values are not slogans. They are the small set of principles already steering how you spend your time, what you say yes to, and where you quietly abandon yourself.

This guide is the whole path in one place. Four simple moves (Uncover, Name, Decide, and Live) plus a short realign step for when life pulls you off course. You are not inventing new values. You are getting clear on the ones already there, and learning to actually live by them.

{# --- 1. What core values are + Why this works --- #}

What Core Values Actually Are

Core values are the handful of principles that shape how you think, act, and decide. Not trends, not inspirational quotes, but deeply personal anchors that tell you when to say yes and when to say no. Psychologists who study values find that most of us are guided by a small, fairly stable set of them, and that naming them clearly is what makes everyday decisions easier and less conflicted.

That is the whole idea of a Values Compass: you are not building new values from scratch. You are surfacing the ones already steering you, and then choosing to honour them on purpose.

Why This Works

Clarity beats willpower. When you know what matters, you stop chasing external validation and start responding to life instead of coping with it. Values give you a way to weigh decisions without guilt or endless overthinking, a calm reference point when everything feels noisy.

{# --- 2. Signs --- #}

Signs You're Living Out of Alignment With Your Values

Misalignment rarely announces itself. It leaks out as low-level resentment, overcommitment, or a sense of running on autopilot. See how many of these sound familiar:

  • You chase goals that look good on paper but feel hollow once you reach them.
  • You say yes when you want to say no, then feel drained or resentful.
  • You keep busy, but underneath it you feel restless or vaguely "off."
  • You know you "should" have boundaries, but they collapse the moment pressure rises.
  • You've done a values exercise before, but the words never seemed to stick.
  • Your calendar is full of obligations and almost nothing you actually chose.

None of these mean anything is wrong with you. They are signals, and a quick way to test one is to compare your week's schedule with what you say matters. If your time goes entirely to obligations rather than priorities, that gap is where the work begins.

{# --- 3. Uncover --- #}

Uncover

Notice What Actually Matters to You

You do not find your values by staring at a list of virtues. You find them by looking at your own life, at the moments you felt most like yourself and the moments you felt most compromised. Behaviour reveals values more honestly than ideals do.

  • List moments from the past year when you felt proud, energised, or deeply content.
  • List the moments you felt frustrated, depleted, or out of alignment, and why.
  • Underline the words and themes that keep repeating across both lists.
  • Notice what you say yes to out of habit, and where you consistently abandon yourself.

Your first shortlist of values usually emerges straight out of those patterns, no theory required.

{# --- 4. Name + free tool callout --- #}

Name

Name Your Values in Your Own Words

A long list of clues becomes a compass the moment you narrow it down and put it in your own language. Aim for three to five values, enough to give direction, few enough to actually remember under pressure.

  • Condense your repeating themes into a shortlist of five values.
  • Write each one in plain language, "family dinners," not "community."
  • Test the list: "If I had to live by only these next month, would my life feel more like mine?"
  • Keep it somewhere visible so it steers real choices, not a drawer.

{# --- 5. Decide --- #}

Decide

Let Your Values Make the Call

A named value is only worth something when it changes a decision. This is where the compass earns its keep, turning "I don't know what to do" into a clear yes, no, or not now.

From Diane

For a long time I could tell how my week had gone by how much praise had landed in it, which meant my sense of things was never really mine, it was on loan. Building an inner sense of whether something had gone well, independent of who noticed, took far longer than I expected and changed far more than I expected too. My values are what I check against now, instead of the applause.

  • Write one simple decision rule: "If it aligns with [value], it's a yes. If it competes, it's a no or a later."
  • Test each new commitment against your values before you agree to it.
  • Ask of any choice: "Does this strengthen or weaken the life I'm trying to build?"
  • Keep a ready script so you can say no without a paragraph of justification.

{# --- 6. Live --- #}

Live

Design Habits and Days Around Your Values

Values stay abstract until they show up in your calendar. Living them is not a personality overhaul, it's a set of small, repeated touchpoints that keep your days pointing the right way.

From Diane

The habit that actually changed my days wasn't a big one. It was reaching for my notebook before my phone in the morning, which sounds small until you notice how much of a day gets decided by whatever the first ten minutes are spent on. I didn't plan that habit consciously at first. I just noticed it was working, and kept doing it.

  • Pick one value and design a ten-minute habit that expresses it ("When I pour my coffee, then I write one line on what matters today").
  • Translate each value into a single visible daily action, small enough to survive a busy day.
  • Protect high-energy windows for the values that need you at your best.
  • End the day with a one-minute check: "Did today reflect what matters to me?"

{# --- 7. Realign --- #}

Realign When Life Pulls You Off Course

Even with the best intentions, drift happens. A short, regular check-in keeps you on course without the drama of "starting over", and reminds you that your values can evolve as you do.

  • Run a weekly Keep / Change / Stop review, fifteen minutes, same day each week.
  • Add one or two tiny experiments to test for the next seven days.
  • Once a month, ask whether how you're honouring each value still fits the season you're in.
  • Treat a shifting value as growth, not inconsistency, reassess rather than force the old version.

From Diane

My own values shifted noticeably somewhere in my forties, quietly and without any single dramatic moment. Things I'd have said mattered a decade earlier just didn't hold the same weight anymore, and other things I'd barely noticed before moved right to the front. Checking in regularly is how I caught that, instead of living for years by a version of my values that had already changed.

FAQ

How do I know what my core values actually are?

Start with behaviour, not ideals. Look at how you spend your time, where you feel most alive, and where you feel most compromised. Those patterns reveal your values more honestly than a list of virtues. The free Core Values Compass helps you sort them into a clear top three.

How many core values should I focus on?

Aim for three to five. That's enough to give direction without diluting your focus. When too many values compete for attention, decisions get harder, a short list of anchors keeps things clear.

Can my values change as I get older?

Yes. Values naturally evolve with experience as your responsibilities and perspective change. Rather than seeing that as inconsistency, treat it as growth. Reassessing your values every so often keeps your choices matched to who you're becoming, not who you used to be.

Why don't my values ever seem to stick?

Usually because they're borrowed words rather than your own, and they never make it into a decision. Name them in plain language, write one simple decision rule, and attach each value to a small daily habit. Values stick when you act on them, not when you write them down.
